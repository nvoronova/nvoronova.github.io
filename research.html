Research

I study quantum computation when resources are tight. Below are my papers, grouped by the question they are about, with a short plain-language explanation for each. If you just want the list, switch to “All papers”.

Authors are in alphabetical order, as usual in theory. Also on dblp and arXiv.

Quantum streaming

What can a few qubits remember that a huge classical memory cannot?

In the streaming model the input goes past once and is far too large to store. The question is how much memory you need to answer something about it. For some problems a small quantum memory is exponentially better than any classical one, and for others it does not help at all. I look for natural problems with a quantum advantage, build tools that make quantum streaming algorithms easier to design, and study how the order in which the input arrives changes the answer.

Explain it simply

Someone reads you a very long list, once, and you only have a tiny notebook. You cannot write everything down, but later you will be asked a question about the whole list. Which questions can you still answer?

A quantum notebook works differently from a paper one. A few qubits can hold a summary that mixes many items together. You can only read a little of it back, but for some questions that little is exactly what you need. Then a handful of qubits does the job of an enormous paper notebook.

  1. Random Order in Quantum Streaming: Replenishment and Robust Lower Boundssolo!

    Nadezhda Voronova

    Preprint, 2026

    arXiv PDF

    In simple words

    Answering a question can use up a quantum memory. If the data arrives in random order, the same kind of information keeps coming back, so the algorithm can refill its memory on the way, like refilling a water bottle at every fountain instead of carrying a big tank. I give a problem where this makes the quantum memory tiny, while without random order, or without quantum memory, you need much more. The paper also shows problems where random order does not help.

  2. How to Design a Quantum Streaming Algorithm Without Knowing Anything About Quantum Computing

    John Kallaugher, Ojas Parekh, Nadezhda Voronova

    SOSA 2025

    arXiv

    In simple words

    A recipe. Take a classical streaming algorithm, replace one part of it with a small quantum building block, and you get a quantum algorithm that uses much less memory. You never have to think about quantum mechanics while doing it. Several earlier quantum advantages follow this way.

  3. Exponential Quantum Space Advantage for Approximating Maximum Directed Cut in the Streaming Model

    John Kallaugher, Ojas Parekh, Nadezhda Voronova

    STOC 2024. Talk at QIP 2024.

    arXiv ECCC

    In simple words

    A huge network of one-way links arrives one link at a time. You want to estimate how well the nodes can be split into two groups so that many links point from the first group to the second. With a few qubits you can get a good estimate, while any classical algorithm needs exponentially more memory. This was the first problem of this natural kind with such a gap.

Communication games and polynomials

Where does quantum advantage start, and where does it stop?

Most memory lower bounds for streaming come from communication games, where one player sends a single message to another. Quantum query lower bounds come from polynomials, through the degree needed to approximate a function. I use these tools, especially sign degree and approximate degree, to find exactly which problems allow a quantum advantage and how large it can be.

Explain it simply

Alice holds a long string of bits. Bob gets to ask one question about it, and Alice may send him only one message, before she knows the question. How long must the message be? If a quantum message can be much shorter than any ordinary one, that usually means quantum memory helps in streaming too.

Polynomials enter because the success probability of a quantum algorithm is a low-degree polynomial in its input. So if a problem cannot be described by a low-degree polynomial, no fast quantum algorithm exists.

  1. Characterizing Quantum Advantage for Generalizations of the Boolean Hidden Matching Problem

    Mark Bun, Joao F. Doriguello, John Kallaugher, Nadezhda Voronova

    Preprint, 2026

    arXiv PDF

    In simple words

    In a classic communication game, Bob checks whether pairs of Alice’s bits are equal or different, and a quantum message can be exponentially shorter than a classical one. We replace “equal or different” with any rule on small groups of bits. One number attached to the rule, its sign degree, tells you how much a quantum message helps. This settles a conjecture from 2020.

  2. Approximate Degree Lower Bounds for Oracle Identification Problems

    Mark Bun, Nadezhda Voronova

    TQC 2023

    arXiv Talk (20 min)

    In simple words

    A hidden word is chosen from a known dictionary, and you find it by asking about its letters. We prove how many questions a quantum algorithm needs for several such problems, using polynomials.

Quantum query complexity

How many questions does a quantum algorithm need to ask about its input?

In the query model an algorithm learns about its input only by asking questions about it, and we count the questions. I am interested in tight bounds for continuous problems, such as convex optimization and linear algebra, where quantum algorithms were known but whether they were optimal was open.

Explain it simply

Think of the game of twenty questions. The algorithm cannot look at its input directly. It can only ask an oracle yes-or-no style questions. A quantum algorithm is allowed to ask about many possibilities at once, and the question is how much that saves. Proving that no algorithm can do better than some number of questions is the kind of result I like most.

  1. Near-Optimal Quantum Lower Bounds for Convex Optimization via Fourier Rank

    Brandon Augustino, Shouvanik Chakrabarti, Enrico Fontana, Dylan Herman, Junhyung Lyle Kim, Guneykan Ozgul, Nadezhda Voronova

    Preprint, 2026

    arXiv PDF

    In simple words

    You want the best point of a convex region in n dimensions, but you can only ask “is this point inside?”. Quantum algorithms for this need about n questions, and we show that you cannot do much better. On the way we show that computing the determinant of a matrix also needs many questions, using a new version of the polynomial method.

Learning with little memory

What can you learn if you cannot remember much?

Some learning tasks become very hard when the learner has little memory, even if it sees many examples. I am interested in what changes when the memory is quantum, and in using such hardness for security, against adversaries who cannot store much.

Explain it simply

If you could only keep a few notes, some patterns would be impossible to learn, no matter how many examples you see. That sounds like bad news, but it can be useful. A secret can be safe from someone who simply cannot remember enough to work it out.
