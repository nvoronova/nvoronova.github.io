Research

I study quantum computation when resources are tight: little memory, few questions, short messages.

What I work on

Quantum memory in streaming

In the streaming model the data goes past once and is too large to store. I ask when a small quantum memory can do the work of a large classical one. With John Kallaugher and Ojas Parekh we found the first natural streaming problem with an exponential quantum advantage, approximating Max Directed Cut. We then turned the main idea into a simple quantum building block that can be plugged into otherwise classical algorithms. Most recently I looked at how the order of the input matters. When the data arrives in random order, a quantum memory that has been used up can be refilled from later data.

Explain it simply

Someone reads you a very long list, once, and you only have a tiny notebook. You cannot write everything down, but later you will be asked a question about the whole list. Which questions can you still answer?

A quantum notebook works differently from a paper one. A few qubits can hold a summary that mixes many items together. You can only read a little of it back, but for some questions that little is exactly what you need. Then a handful of qubits does the job of an enormous paper notebook.

Communication: where the limits come from

To show that quantum memory does not help, or helps only a little, one usually goes through a communication game. Alice sends Bob a single message, and we ask how long it must be. The best-known example is Boolean Hidden Matching, where quantum messages are exponentially shorter than classical ones. With Mark Bun, Joao Doriguello and John Kallaugher we found exactly which generalizations of this game keep a quantum advantage, in terms of the sign degree of the function involved. My solo paper also proves new quantum communication lower bounds, for Multiple Hidden Matching and Block Hidden XOR, and uses them for streaming in random order.

Explain it simply

Alice holds a long string of bits and may send Bob one message. Then Bob has to answer a question about her string. How long must the message be? If even a quantum message has to be long, then a streaming algorithm also needs a lot of quantum memory, because its memory is exactly the message the first half of the stream sends to the second half.

Counting questions: queries and polynomials

In the query model an algorithm sees its input only by asking questions about it, and we count the questions. The polynomial method turns this into a question about polynomials. With Mark Bun I used approximate degree to prove tight lower bounds for oracle identification problems. In a larger collaboration we showed that high-accuracy convex optimization needs about n quantum queries in n dimensions, so the known quantum algorithms are optimal up to logarithmic factors. The key step there is a new form of the polynomial method, for computing the determinant of a real matrix.

Explain it simply

Think of the game of twenty questions. The algorithm cannot look at its input directly, it can only ask questions about it. A quantum algorithm may ask about many possibilities at once, and the question is how much that saves.

Polynomials enter because the answer of a quantum algorithm that asks few questions is described by a low-degree polynomial in its input. If a problem cannot be described by a low-degree polynomial, there is no quantum algorithm for it that asks few questions.

Papers

Authors are in alphabetical order, as usual in theory. Also on dblp and arXiv.

  1. Random Order in Quantum Streaming: Replenishment and Robust Lower Boundssolo!

    Nadezhda Voronova

    Preprint, 2026

    StreamingCommunication

    arXiv PDF

    In simple words

    Answering a question can use up a quantum memory. If the data arrives in random order, the same kind of information keeps coming back, so the algorithm can refill its memory on the way, like refilling a water bottle at every fountain instead of carrying a big tank. I give a problem where this makes the quantum memory tiny, while without random order, or without quantum memory, you need much more. The paper also shows problems where random order does not help.

  2. Characterizing Quantum Advantage for Generalizations of the Boolean Hidden Matching Problem

    Mark Bun, Joao F. Doriguello, John Kallaugher, Nadezhda Voronova

    Preprint, 2026

    CommunicationPolynomials

    arXiv PDF

    In simple words

    In a classic communication game, Bob checks whether pairs of Alice’s bits are equal or different, and a quantum message can be exponentially shorter than a classical one. We replace “equal or different” with any rule on small groups of bits. One number attached to the rule, its sign degree, tells you how much a quantum message helps. This settles a conjecture from 2020.

  3. Near-Optimal Quantum Lower Bounds for Convex Optimization via Fourier Rank

    Brandon Augustino, Shouvanik Chakrabarti, Enrico Fontana, Dylan Herman, Junhyung Lyle Kim, Guneykan Ozgul, Nadezhda Voronova

    Preprint, 2026

    QueriesPolynomials

    arXiv PDF

    In simple words

    You want the best point of a convex region in n dimensions, but you can only ask “is this point inside?”. Quantum algorithms for this need about n questions, and we show that you cannot do much better. On the way we show that computing the determinant of a matrix also needs many questions, using a new version of the polynomial method.

  4. How to Design a Quantum Streaming Algorithm Without Knowing Anything About Quantum Computing

    John Kallaugher, Ojas Parekh, Nadezhda Voronova

    SOSA 2025

    Streaming

    arXiv

    In simple words

    A recipe. Take a classical streaming algorithm, replace one part of it with a small quantum building block, and you get a quantum algorithm that uses much less memory. You never have to think about quantum mechanics while doing it. Several earlier quantum advantages follow this way.

  5. Exponential Quantum Space Advantage for Approximating Maximum Directed Cut in the Streaming Model

    John Kallaugher, Ojas Parekh, Nadezhda Voronova

    STOC 2024. Talk at QIP 2024.

    Streaming

    arXiv ECCC

    In simple words

    A huge network of one-way links arrives one link at a time. You want to estimate how well the nodes can be split into two groups so that many links point from the first group to the second. With a few qubits you can get a good estimate, while any classical algorithm needs exponentially more memory. This was the first problem of this natural kind with such a gap.

  6. Approximate Degree Lower Bounds for Oracle Identification Problems

    Mark Bun, Nadezhda Voronova

    arXiv Talk (20 min)

    In simple words

    A hidden word is chosen from a known dictionary, and you find it by asking about its letters. We prove how many questions a quantum algorithm needs for several such problems, using polynomials.
