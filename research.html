Research

I study quantum computation when resources are tight: little memory, few questions, short messages.

What I work on

Quantum streaming: computing with small quantum memory

A streaming algorithm processes a long sequence of updates in one pass using memory much smaller than the input. A quantum streaming algorithm processes the same classical stream but maintains a small number of qubits. The central question is when a few qubits can replace a much larger classical memory. Such advantages are unconditional, since they are proved through communication complexity, and they require a quantum device only as large as the memory. With Kallaugher and Parekh I gave the first exponential quantum space advantage for a natural streaming problem, approximating Maximum Directed Cut, and isolated the quantum sketch behind this and earlier advantages. In recent single-author work I study how quantum memory interacts with the order of the stream. I show that all four combinations of classical or quantum memory with adversarial or random order are exponentially separated, and I identify two properties of quantum memory that govern this: consumability, the fact that a measurement destroys the state it reads, and replenishment, the ability to rebuild that state from data that keeps arriving.

In simple words

Quantum computers will have very little memory for a long time. So a natural question is what a small quantum memory is good for. Streaming is the cleanest way to ask it. A huge amount of ordinary data flows past once, and the computer can keep only a small summary. I study when a summary made of qubits can be exponentially smaller than any summary made of bits, and when it cannot. The answers depend on surprising details, such as the order in which the data arrives and the fact that looking at a quantum summary destroys it.

Quantum communication: what one quantum message can do

In one-way communication complexity, Alice holds part of an input and sends a single message to Bob, who must compute a function of both parts. This model is the basic case of computation on distributed data, and it is the main source of lower bounds for streaming. Exponential separations between quantum and classical messages are known, but almost all of them rest on one problem, Boolean Hidden Matching. With Bun, Doriguello and Kallaugher I characterized a broad generalization, the $f$-Boolean Hidden Partition problems. The classical one-way complexity is $\Theta(n^{1-1/d})$ and the quantum complexity lies between $\Omega(n^{1-2/d})$ and $\tilde{O}(n^{1-1/\lceil d/2 \rceil})$, where $d$ is the sign degree of $f$. This proves a conjecture of Doriguello and Montanaro and shows exactly which problems of this family admit a quantum advantage.

In simple words

Sometimes data is split between two machines, and sending information between them is the expensive part. A quantum message can sometimes carry what is needed in exponentially fewer qubits than the bits a classical message would need. But this happens only for special problems, and for a long time we had essentially one example. I work on understanding which problems allow such savings and which do not. The same understanding tells us where to look for quantum advantages in streaming and, in the future, in networks of small quantum computers.

Quantum query complexity: proving that speedups stop somewhere

In the query model, an algorithm accesses its input only through queries to an oracle, and the cost is the number of queries. Most known quantum speedups are query speedups, and query lower bounds are the main way to prove that a quantum algorithm is optimal. I work on lower-bound techniques, in particular on the polynomial method. With coauthors at JPMorgan Chase I introduced Fourier rank, an extension of the polynomial method to real-valued inputs, and used it to prove an $\Omega(n)$ lower bound for convex optimization with membership queries, which matches the known quantum algorithms, together with tight bounds for determinant and eigenvalue problems with matrix-vector queries. With Bun I proved an $\Omega(n / \log^2 n)$ lower bound on the approximate degree of ordered search and of hidden string identification, where the polynomial method was previously known to give only $\Omega(\sqrt{n})$.

In simple words

A lot of excitement about quantum computers comes from problems where they need to look at far less of the input than classical computers do. It is just as important to know where this stops. If we can prove that no quantum algorithm can do better than the one we have, people can stop searching and can make honest estimates of what quantum computers will deliver. I develop the mathematical tools for such proofs, and I have used them to show that the known quantum algorithms for convex optimization and for searching a sorted list are essentially the best possible.

  1. Four Ways to Stream: Exponential Separations from Random Order and Quantum Memory

    Nadezhda Voronova

    In submission, 2026

    StreamingCommunication

    arXiv PDF

    Abstract

    How can random order change the role of quantum memory in streaming? Later classical input can restore the usefulness of a quantum state consumed by earlier queries. We call this replenishment.

    We construct an artificial problem based on Hidden Matching, with repeated coordinate data and online matching requests. It admits a one-pass quantum algorithm using polylogarithmic space in uniformly random order, but unconditionally requires polynomial space both classically in random order and quantumly when all updates precede the requests.

    To prove the quantum lower bound, we strengthen the consumability bounds of Gilboa, Jain, and McClean for Multiple Hidden Matching. Without prior entanglement, any quantum encoding supporting $r$ independent matching requests requires $\Omega(r)$ qubits for $r\le N^{1/2-\delta}$ and every fixed $\delta\in(0,1/2)$, even with simultaneous revelation and arbitrary joint decoding. For sequential requests, the linear bound extends through $r=\Theta(\sqrt N)$.

    We also adapt Kallaugher's triangle-counting algorithm to uniformly random streams in which every edge is repeated equally often. Rebuilding the quantum sketch and resampling the classical estimator improve its expected-space bound in suitable parameter regimes.

    Finally, we extend the robust Noisy Gap Cycle framework of Assadi and Sundaresan to quantum streaming. A quantum communication lower bound for Block Hidden XOR yields an $\Omega(n)$ space lower bound in random edge order for large enough cycles, with consequences for several graph problems.

    Thus random order can enable replenishment of small quantum representations, while substantial space requirements persist for other tasks.

    In simple words

    A streaming algorithm reads a huge input once, piece by piece, and can remember only a little. Two things are known to make such algorithms much stronger. One is quantum memory. The other is luck with the order: the pieces arrive in random order, not in the worst possible one. This paper asks how the two interact. The answer is that anything can happen. There are problems where each one helps and the other does not, problems where neither helps, and a problem where only the two together help. The last case comes from a property of quantum memory that has no classical counterpart. Reading a quantum state destroys it, so a quantum memory can answer only a few questions before it is used up. If the data keeps arriving in random order, the algorithm can rebuild the state from the new data and keep answering. I call this replenishment.

  2. Characterizing Quantum Advantage for Generalizations of the Boolean Hidden Matching Problem

    Mark Bun, Joao F. Doriguello, John Kallaugher, Nadezhda Voronova

    In submission, 2026

    CommunicationPolynomials

    arXiv PDF

    Abstract

    We study the one-way communication complexity of the $f$-Boolean Hidden Partition problem. Here, Alice is given an $n$-bit string and Bob is given $\Omega(n)$ disjoint blocks of its indices together with a string of labels. Under the promise that the evaluations of $f$ on these blocks either agree with all of the labels or disagree with all of them, Bob must determine which is the case using a single message from Alice. This problem generalizes the Boolean Hidden Matching and Hidden Hypermatching problems, which capture the special case where $f$ is the parity function.

    We establish classical and quantum communication bounds for $f$-Boolean Hidden Partition in terms of the sign degree $d$ of $f$, proving a conjecture of Doriguello and Montanaro (TQC 2020). Their prior work gave logarithmic communication upper bounds for classical protocols when $d \le 1$ and quantum protocols when $d \le 2$, as well as polynomial lower bounds for certain structured functions with larger sign degree. We show that for every $f$ of sign degree $d \ge 2$, the randomized classical communication complexity of this problem is $\Theta(n^{1-1/d})$ while its quantum communication complexity lies between $\Omega(n^{1-2/d})$ and $O(n^{1-1/\lceil d/2 \rceil} \log n)$. This completely characterizes the functions $f$ admitting polynomial quantum advantage as those for which $d \ge 2$, with a new infinite family of exponential separations given by the case $d = 2$.

    In simple words

    Alice has a long string and can send Bob one message. How short can the message be if Bob needs to learn one specific thing about the string? For a famous problem called Boolean Hidden Matching, a quantum message can be exponentially shorter than any classical message. This problem is the engine behind most known quantum advantages in communication and in streaming, but it is a single example. We study a whole family of problems of the same kind, where the task Bob has to solve on each small block of the string is given by an arbitrary function. We show that one number attached to this function, its sign degree, decides everything: how long a classical message must be, how long a quantum message must be, and whether quantum helps at all. This proves a conjecture of Doriguello and Montanaro.

  3. Near-Optimal Quantum Lower Bounds for Convex Optimization via Fourier Rank

    Brandon Augustino, Shouvanik Chakrabarti, Enrico Fontana, Dylan Herman, Junhyung Lyle Kim, Guneykan Ozgul, Nadezhda Voronova

    In submission, 2026

    QueriesPolynomials

    arXiv PDF

    Abstract

    We establish a linear quantum query lower bound for high-accuracy convex optimization over an explicit family of $n$-dimensional ellipsoids. We focus on linear optimization with an explicitly given objective, where the feasible set is accessed through a membership oracle. We show that any algorithm that, for every unit linear objective, returns an exactly feasible point with additive objective error $\Theta(n^{-1/2})$ requires $\Omega(n)$ membership queries, where the ellipsoids are contained in the unit ball. The same lower bound can be shown to hold if the returned point is only required to be approximately feasible, within $\Theta(n^{-3/2})$ distance from the feasible set. This resolves an open question posed by Chakrabarti, Childs, Li, and Wu (Quantum, 2020) and by van Apeldoorn, Gilyén, Gribling, and de Wolf (Quantum, 2020). Coupled with the upper bounds in these papers, the query complexity of high-accuracy convex optimization is characterized tightly up to logarithmic factors. The proof is built around a lower bound for determinant computation that is derived via a novel polynomial method based on Fourier-rank. In the continuous matrix phase-query model, computing the determinant of a real $n\times n$ matrix requires at least $n/2$ matrix-vector product queries. The construction also yields an $\Omega(n)$ phase-query lower bound for estimating the minimum eigenvalue of a real symmetric $n\times n$ matrix to additive accuracy $\Theta(n^{-2})$. These results extend the determinant and minimum-eigenvalue lower bounds of Childs, Hung, and Li (ICALP 2021) from finite fields to the real-valued setting. Based on the same constructions, we also prove an $\Omega(\min\{\sqrt\kappa,n\})$ gradient-query lower bound for constant-accuracy optimization of $1$-strongly convex and $\kappa$-smooth functions, which is optimal up to a logarithmic factor.

    In simple words

    Convex optimization is one of the applications people hope quantum computers will speed up. An algorithm for it learns about the problem by asking questions such as “is this point allowed?” Quantum algorithms were known that need about n such questions in dimension n, while classical algorithms need about n². Could a smarter quantum algorithm get by with far fewer questions, say √n? We prove that it cannot. About n questions are necessary, so the known quantum algorithms are essentially the best possible. The standard tool for results of this kind works for inputs made of bits and does not apply to problems with real numbers. We extend it with a new measure that we call Fourier rank, and with it we also prove limits for computing determinants and eigenvalues.

  4. How to Design a Quantum Streaming Algorithm Without Knowing Anything About Quantum Computing

    John Kallaugher, Ojas Parekh, Nadezhda Voronova

    SOSA 2025. Poster at QIP 2025.

    Streaming

    arXiv

    Abstract on arXiv
    In simple words

    Quantum streaming algorithms are hard to design and hard to read, because the people who know streaming usually do not know quantum computing. We show that the quantum part of the known algorithms is always the same small component: a sketch that samples pairs of items from the stream in a way no classical memory of the same size can. We describe this component once, with guarantees stated in purely classical language. After that, a quantum streaming algorithm is an ordinary classical algorithm that calls this component as a black box. The three known quantum streaming advantages of this type become short and simple in this form, and anyone who knows classical algorithms can now design new ones.

  5. Exponential Quantum Space Advantage for Approximating Maximum Directed Cut in the Streaming Model

    John Kallaugher, Ojas Parekh, Nadezhda Voronova

    STOC 2024. Talk at QIP 2024.

    Streaming

    arXiv ECCC

    Abstract on arXiv
    In simple words

    Imagine a huge directed graph whose edges fly past once, and a computer that can remember only a tiny fraction of them. The task is to estimate the size of the best “directed cut” of the graph, a classic optimization problem. A classical computer with small memory cannot do better than a certain accuracy (a 4/9 fraction of the true answer) unless its memory grows like the square root of the graph size. We give a quantum algorithm that beats this accuracy with exponentially less memory, only polylogarithmically many qubits. It was the first exponential quantum memory advantage for a natural streaming problem, one that people cared about before anyone asked about quantum. It is also the first proven exponential quantum advantage of any kind for approximating a discrete optimization problem.

  6. Approximate Degree Lower Bounds for Oracle Identification Problems

    Mark Bun, Nadezhda Voronova

    TQC 2023

    QueriesPolynomials

    arXiv Talk (20 min)

    Abstract on arXiv
    In simple words

    Binary search finds a number between 1 and N with about log N comparisons. A quantum computer can do slightly better, but only by a constant factor, and this has been known for a long time. The known proof, however, uses a technique whose results do not carry over to other settings, such as communication or algorithms with tiny success probability. The more robust technique, the polynomial method, gave only a much weaker bound for this problem. We close most of this gap. We show that the polynomial method gives a nearly optimal bound for ordered search and for a related problem of finding a hidden string. The proof takes an unusual route: it turns efficient communication protocols into limits on query algorithms.
